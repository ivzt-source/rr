# Skit ad format (solo multi-character)

Produce a comedic sketch ad where **one recurring "performer identity" plays every
character** — the format Jimmy Rees ("Meanwhile in Australia", "The Guy Who Decides",
"The Brighton Ladies") and Trey Kennedy built audiences of millions on. In our pipeline
the "performer" is an **AI-generated character** (or a small stable of them), not a
human. This skill is the repeatable step-by-step for turning a topic + a product into a
finished script and shot list.

Every rule below is grounded in the research dossier at
`../ad-formats-research/RESEARCH-DOSSIER.md` — read it if you want the citations. The
short version of what the evidence says:

- Build the sketch around **exactly one comedic idea** ("the game") and exhaust it.
- Structure is **Set-up → Reveal → Escalation → Payoff** (SREP); each escalation beat
  should be *surprising in the moment but inevitable in hindsight*.
- The format's durable equity comes from **recurring named characters + catchphrases +
  repeatable setups**, not from any single viral post.
- **Topicality (news cycle) accelerates reach; recurring-character franchises create
  the durable audience.** Use both, but don't depend on news alone.
- With AI characters you **lose the "wow, it's all one guy" meta-joke** — the single
  biggest thing you must consciously replace (see "AI-character adjustments").

---

## Step 0 — Decide the job of this specific video

Pick one, before writing a word:

| Goal | What the video optimizes for |
|---|---|
| **Reach / top-of-funnel** | A topical or highly relatable premise; product barely present or absent. Builds the character's audience. |
| **Conversion** | Product is *organically* the resolution of the sketch; clear payoff/CTA. |
| **Franchise-building** | A recurring character in a repeatable setup; catchphrase reinforced. |

You cannot maximize all three in 30 seconds. A healthy channel runs mostly reach/
franchise videos and periodically "cashes in" attention with a conversion video.

---

## Step 1 — Find the one game

A sketch is *one unusual thing that heightens to comedic absurdity*. Write the game as a
single sentence in the form:

> **"In a world where [normal situation], [one character] insists on [one absurd rule/
> behaviour] — and it keeps getting worse."**

Examples of a well-formed game:
- "The guy who single-handedly decides every baffling product-design choice."
- "Every Australian state premier negotiating lockdown like squabbling siblings."
- "The manager who is technically agreeing with you while denying your request."

Tests before you proceed:
1. **One idea only.** If you can't state it in one sentence, it's two sketches.
2. **Relatable core.** The audience must recognize the *normal* situation instantly.
   Relatability is the engagement nexus even for elevated comedy.
3. **Escalatable.** The absurd behaviour must have obvious "worse" versions.

---

## Step 2 — Cast the characters (2–4 max)

Solo multi-character comedy is a **two-hander at heart**: usually a *straight character*
(reasonable, audience surrogate) and an *unreasonable character* (the engine of the
game). Add 1–2 more only if each brings a distinct escalation.

For **each** character, lock these before generating anything — this is the AI-critical
step, because you no longer get "same face, different wig" recognition for free:

- **Name** (recurring characters must be named and reused).
- **One-line archetype** ("the over-caffeinated middle manager").
- **Visual markers**: wardrobe, colour, framing/position, prop. Must be readable in
  <1 second.
- **Vocal markers**: accent, pitch, cadence, a verbal tic or catchphrase.
- **Screen position rule**: give each character a consistent side/height/background so
  hard cuts read as "different person" instantly.

> Clear visual **or** vocal distinction between roles (costume, accent, mannerism) is
> the single stated best practice for solo multi-character skits. With AI, over-index
> here: exaggerate the deltas more than a human actor would need to.

---

## Step 3 — Write to the SREP beat sheet

Four beats. Target 15–45s for feed ads (the format also scales to multi-minute branded
pieces — Rees's Google "Meanwhile in Agency Land" ran ~4 min — but default short).

1. **SET-UP (0–3s).** Establish the normal situation *without telegraphing the joke*.
   This doubles as your **hook** — see Step 4. Fast. No logo, no preamble.
2. **REVEAL (≈3–7s).** The unusual thing lands — the audience now "gets" the game. This
   is the laugh that earns the next 20 seconds.
3. **ESCALATION (the body).** The game recurs, heightening each time. *Variations on the
   same game, never new premises.* Rule of thumb: **3 escalating beats**, each a notch
   more absurd than the last. Each beat should feel shocking in the moment yet, in
   retrospect, the only thing that could have happened.
4. **PAYOFF (final beats).** Not a one-line "punchline" — a *payoff section*. Two proven
   endings:
   - **Reversal**: the straight character finally adopts the unreasonable character's
     logic (turns the tables / gives in / out-absurds them).
   - **Product-as-resolution**: the product/offer is the thing that actually solves or
     detonates the absurdity. This is where a conversion video earns its CTA.

Scripting constraints:
- Introduce **no totally new comedic ideas after the Reveal.** Everything is the game.
- Keep the straight character genuinely reasonable — the contrast *is* the comedy.

---

## Step 4 — Hook the first 1–2 seconds

Short-form lives or dies in the first two seconds. Open on the strongest of:

- **Outrage / curiosity first line** (highest performing pattern):
  - "I caught my roommate paying for 3 Netflix accounts?!"
  - "I got fired for saving people too much money."
  - "Apparently *I'm* the one who decides what buttons do."
- **In-progress absurdity**: drop the viewer mid-scene, no setup.
- **Named-franchise cold open**: recurring audience already knows the character; the
  title card / catchphrase *is* the hook.

Never open with a logo, a slow pan, or "Hey guys." The Reveal can come as early as
second one for a known franchise.

---

## Step 5 — Storyboard the shot list

Framing and editing dominate perceived quality far more than camera resolution. Keep it
simple and legible; there is **no single mandatory shot grammar** (a common "wide →
medium → close-up, or it looks cheap" rule did not survive fact-checking) — the real
rules are:

- **One clean, consistent frame per character** so cuts read instantly (Step 2's screen-
  position rule). Consistency matters more than coverage.
- **Close-ups for elevated emotion / the payoff.**
- **Light the face** (for AI gen: prompt front/soft key light, avoid backlight and murky
  interiors — murk reads as "cheap/AI-broken").
- **Legible, uncluttered backgrounds** that reinforce each character's identity.

Output a beat-by-beat shot list: `[beat] — [character] — [frame] — [line] — [action]`.

## Step 6 — Time it in the edit

Comedic timing is created in the **edit**, not the performance:

- Cut **a few frames after** a character finishes the line (use the audio waveform to
  find the stop). Don't leave dead air; don't clip the last syllable.
- The **hard cut between characters** is the comedic beat — let the funniest reactions
  breathe by a beat, rush the setup lines.
- For AI-generated clips: generate each character's line as a separate take, then
  assemble; this gives you frame-level control the model won't give you in one pass.

---

## Step 7 — Integrate the product without breaking the game

For conversion videos only:
- Product enters **as part of the story**, ideally as the Payoff's resolution, not bolted
  on after the sketch ends.
- The sketch's *relatable problem* should be the problem the product solves. The
  cleanest structure: everyday relatable situation → product organically in the story →
  strong resolution (twist or CTA).
- Keep the sell inside the world. A recurring character selling in-character out-performs
  a "and now a word from our sponsor" hard pivot.

---

## Step 8 — Franchise & cadence (the part that compounds)

The single biggest lever for durable performance is **consistency**:
- Reuse characters, catchphrases, and setups so audiences learn to recognize and seek
  them out. Recurring franchises (not one-offs) are what built Rees's and Kennedy's
  audiences.
- **Post on a consistent cadence.** Inconsistency doesn't just stall growth — it erodes
  audience trust. Decide a rhythm the AI pipeline can actually sustain and hold it.
- Keep a **character bible** (names, markers, catchphrases, canonical backgrounds) so
  every generation is on-model. This is non-negotiable for AI characters — model drift
  between videos will silently kill recognition.

---

## AI-character adjustments (read this — it's what makes or breaks the format)

The classic format's charm is partly the meta-joke: *one real human plays everyone*.
**AI characters don't get that laugh for free.** Compensate deliberately:

1. **Replace "same person" recognition with "same *character*" recognition.** Lean hard
   on named recurring characters, catchphrases, and repeatable setups. Familiarity
   mechanics still work when the "person" is synthetic — but only if the characters are
   consistent across videos. Maintain the character bible ruthlessly.
2. **Over-differentiate roles.** Push visual/vocal contrast further than a human actor
   would need — AI faces can blur together and lose the "different person" read.
3. **Design a coherent, stable, openly-virtual persona.** The evidence on AI characters
   is *mixed, not damning*: virtual influencers can enhance engagement, and the risks
   (authenticity, uncanny valley, skepticism) are best managed with a consistent,
   transparent digital identity rather than by passing the character off as a real human.
   Don't aim for "fool them into thinking it's real" — aim for "a virtual character they
   like and recognize."
4. **Consider deliberate imperfection.** Flawless, over-polished AI aesthetics can read
   as artificial and cool trust. A slightly lived-in look often beats uncanny perfection.
   (Treat as a design heuristic to A/B, not a law.)
5. **Disclosure is contested — decide per jurisdiction, then commit.** Experimental
   evidence is genuinely mixed: one study found an AI-generated-content label lowered
   trust in the ad and the brand, *but also* found a countervailing path where it raised
   trust via persuasion knowledge; another found the effect depends on what viewers
   believe AI can do. The strong claim that "disclosure always makes it worse" did **not**
   survive fact-checking. Practical stance: check local ad rules (some jurisdictions
   require AI disclosure), and where you disclose, do it as part of a confident, stylized,
   openly-virtual brand rather than as an apologetic asterisk.
6. **Audience matters.** Digitally literate audiences react less negatively to AI
   content — factor that into targeting.

## One-screen checklist

- [ ] One game, stated in a single sentence, with an obvious relatable core
- [ ] Goal chosen (reach / conversion / franchise) and video built for it
- [ ] 2–4 characters, each with name + visual + vocal markers + fixed screen position
- [ ] SREP beats written; 3 escalating beats; no new ideas after the Reveal
- [ ] Hook in first 1–2s (outrage/curiosity line, in-progress absurdity, or franchise cold open)
- [ ] Payoff is a reversal or product-as-resolution (not a bolted-on tag)
- [ ] Shot list: one consistent legible frame per character, face lit, close-up on payoff
- [ ] Edit: cut a few frames after each line; hard cut = the comedic beat
- [ ] Product (if conversion) resolves the sketch's own relatable problem
- [ ] Character bible updated; cadence scheduled; catchphrase reused
- [ ] AI persona coherent/stable; roles over-differentiated; disclosure decision made
